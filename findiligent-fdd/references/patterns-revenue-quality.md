# FinDiligent Pattern Library — Revenue Quality

Version 1.0 · 1 Nov 2026 · Author: CA Ayush Bansal

Twelve patterns that tell a consuming project what a revenue signal might mean, what it would do to earnings, cash and value, and what evidence would settle it. Every pattern is drawn from the book and uses the vocabulary of the four definition docs. Every hypothesis and effect is indicative, not foolproof.

## How to read an entry

Each pattern has the same nine fields, so a consuming project can match a signal and work through it in order.

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | What a consuming project sees in news or filings that matches this pattern |
| Competing hypotheses | Possible explanations, always including a benign one; none is assumed true |
| Financial effects | What each adverse hypothesis would do to revenue, EBITDA, working capital or net debt, in the definitions' vocabulary |
| Possible outcomes | What could follow if it is not resolved |
| Evidence — public | What to check in filings, notes, presentations and calls first |
| Evidence — diligence | What only private data would settle; listed as open questions, never assumed |
| Credit reading | How a lender should read the signal |
| Equity reading | How an equity investor should read it |
| Source | Book pages and origin label |

**Grading.** A consuming project grades each hypothesis Supported, Plausible or Unsupported according to the public evidence it found (D08), and archives unresolved ones after two quarters (D30).

**Order of work.** Verify the claim against filings first (D26); test the benign hypothesis before the adverse ones; record what remains unknown.

## RQ01 — Growth from a non-recurring source

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | Revenue growth well above trend that management or news ties to one contract, one customer, one order or one external event; or a one-quarter step-up |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Genuine new recurring business. (b) A single non-recurring contract, possibly with a related party (p.9). (c) A temporary external tailwind, such as a competitor's supply disruption (p.57). (d) A peak quarter annualised as a run-rate (p.57) |
| Financial effects | Run-rate revenue and EBITDA overstated; the valuation multiple is applied to earnings that will not recur; related-party revenue may convert to cash slowly |
| Possible outcomes | Growth reverses within one to four quarters; value gap equals non-recurring EBITDA × multiple |
| Evidence — public | Related-party transactions note; customers above 10% of revenue (Ind AS 108); quarterly revenue trend; order-book commentary; related-party receivables |
| Evidence — diligence | Customer-wise revenue; contract term and renewal; pricing versus other customers |
| Credit reading | Do not size debt or repayment schedules on the peak year; test DSCR on revenue excluding the one-off |
| Equity reading | Exclude the one-off from run-rate EBITDA; a growth multiple needs evidence of recurrence |
| Source | [Book] pp.9, 57 |

## RQ02 — Price-led growth with flat or falling volume

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | Revenue up while volume (units, patients, students, footfall) is flat or down; management cites price increases |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Genuine pricing power from brand, scarcity or regulation. (b) Price rises on captive customers that will drive attrition (p.55). (c) Mix shift to higher-priced products (p.53). (d) Inflation pass-through with no real growth |
| Financial effects | Margin may rise in the short term while volume and share erode; future revenue more exposed to competitor entry |
| Possible outcomes | Customer churn, volume decline, margin reversal; or durable growth where pricing power is real |
| Evidence — public | Volume KPIs in presentations; competitor pricing and market share; management commentary on churn; sector context (book IVF case: 10% growth from price with flat patients, p.5) |
| Evidence — diligence | SKU-level price-volume analysis (book method, p.55); customer-level retention |
| Credit reading | Price-led EBITDA is less durable; stress test a volume decline of the size the trend implies |
| Equity reading | Apply a lower multiple unless pricing power is evidenced by stable volume after price rises |
| Source | [Book] pp.5, 48, 53, 55 |

## RQ03 — Mix shift that dilutes or flatters margin

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | Blended margin moves while segment, product or specialty mix changes; growth concentrated in one line |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Growth in low-margin lines diluting the blend (book: economy SKUs +35% volume at \~12% margin, p.55). (b) Shift to high-value lines lifting revenue but also costs (high-acuity specialties need dearer consumables and surgeons, p.53). (c) An acquisition changing the mix |
| Financial effects | Blended margin misleads about each line; operating leverage may be absent; sector gross margin (E2) moves differently from material margin |
| Possible outcomes | Continued dilution; or margin gains offset by rising direct costs |
| Evidence — public | Segment reporting; product or specialty revenue split; sector gross margin trend |
| Evidence — diligence | Margin by SKU or specialty; cost-to-serve by line |
| Credit reading | Underwrite on the core line's margin, not the blend |
| Equity reading | Value lines separately; do not capitalise a blended margin that the mix is changing |
| Source | [Book] pp.53, 55 |

## RQ04 — Gross-to-net leakage

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | Gross sales growing faster than net sales; rising discounts, schemes or returns; large year-end rebate programmes or post-period credit notes |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Deliberate trade investment to win share, properly accrued. (b) Growth bought with trade spend, showing weak pricing power. (c) Rebates under-accrued (book: 120 actual vs 50 accrued, p.56). (d) Quality problems or overstocked channels driving returns |
| Financial effects | Revenue and EBITDA overstated by any under-accrual, less attached variable costs such as commissions (E6); rebate liability in NWC understated |
| Possible outcomes | Negative QoE adjustment multiplied into value (book: −70 EBITDA, −700 EV at 10x); post-completion cash claims |
| Evidence — public | Revenue note (gross, discounts, net); returns trend; management commentary on schemes; next quarter's revenue and credit notes |
| Evidence — diligence | Scheme circulars; distributor claims; credit notes issued after year-end |
| Credit reading | Unaccrued rebates are near-term cash claims; treat as debt-like until settled |
| Equity reading | Net realised price, not gross or MRP-based sales, is the true growth measure |
| Source | [Book] p.56 |

## RQ05 — Channel stuffing or period-end push

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | A year-end or festive-quarter revenue spike with a receivables or DSO jump; rising distributor stock; extended credit or year-end schemes; weaker following quarter |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Genuine seasonal demand (festive quarter is Q3 of the fiscal year, p.57). (b) Primary sales pushed ahead of secondary sales (book: primary +45%, secondary +6%, distributor stock 40 → 105 days, p.56). (c) Pipeline fill for new distributors. (d) Pre-buying ahead of a price rise |
| Financial effects | Revenue pulled forward; DSO and receivables up; returns and credit notes later; cash conversion (R2) falls |
| Possible outcomes | Next-period revenue dip; returns; receivable write-offs |
| Evidence — public | September vs March DSO (W3 gap test); quarterly revenue shape over several years; next-quarter returns; management comments on channel inventory |
| Evidence — diligence | Secondary sales; distributor stock; scheme terms; month-end receivable spikes (p.65) |
| Credit reading | Receivables may be overstated as collateral; check borrowing-base eligibility and ageing |
| Equity reading | Normalise the peak; channel stuffing creates no new revenue, it only moves it (p.57) |
| Source | [Book] pp.56–57, 60, 65 |

## RQ06 — Upfront recognition of multi-period services

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | A business sells packages, courses or subscriptions delivered over time, yet contract liabilities are low or falling relative to bookings; revenue tracks sales rather than delivery |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Delivery period genuinely short, so upfront recognition is appropriate. (b) Full package recognised at sale (book: IVF packages, p.5; multi-year course fees, p.52). (c) Refund obligations under-provided (book: \~15% withdrawals in coaching, p.52) |
| Financial effects | Revenue and EBITDA overstated by the change in the unearned balance, less attached variable costs (E6); contract liability understated |
| Possible outcomes | Restatement; revenue reversal; refund cash outflows |
| Evidence — public | Revenue-recognition policy; contract liabilities note; refund policy; growth in bookings vs revenue |
| Evidence — diligence | Package terms and delivery schedule; cohort withdrawal rates over 3–5 years |
| Credit reading | Cash already received funds future delivery; the obligation consumes future cash, so do not count it as free liquidity |
| Equity reading | Adjust earnings by the change in unearned revenue, not the full balance |
| Source | [Book] pp.5–6, 25, 52 |

## RQ07 — Fixed income turning variable

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | Contracts move from fixed or minimum-guarantee terms to revenue share or performance-based fees; renegotiated terms or concessions disclosed |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Strategic move to share upside with strong counterparties. (b) Counterparties under stress from falling footfall or sales (book: anchor tenants moved from MG to revenue share, p.49). (c) Bargaining power shifted to the counterparty |
| Financial effects | Revenue visibility falls; earnings become more volatile; historical guaranteed revenue is no longer representative |
| Possible outcomes | Revenue decline if counterparty sales stay weak; multiple compression |
| Evidence — public | Split of fixed vs variable revenue; occupancy or utilisation; footfall and counterparty sales commentary |
| Evidence — diligence | Contract terms, lock-ins, escalation clauses; quarter-by-quarter MG vs revenue-share income |
| Credit reading | Lower predictability means lower debt capacity and higher covenant risk |
| Equity reading | Adjust maintainable revenue; a lower multiple for variable income |
| Source | [Book] pp.49–50 |

## RQ08 — Renewal and expiry concentration

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | A large share of contracts, leases or licences expiring within 12–24 months, with no consistent renewal record |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Normal renewal cycle with a strong renewal history. (b) At-risk counterparties likely to leave (book: 35 of 56 in-line tenant leases expiring within 12–24 months, p.49). (c) Renewals at lower rates |
| Financial effects | Revenue and occupancy cliff; re-letting costs and downtime |
| Possible outcomes | Revenue drop; liquidity pressure if the cliff meets debt maturities |
| Evidence — public | Expiry or order-book profile in presentations; renewal history; new signings (LOIs) |
| Evidence — diligence | Full expiry schedule; renewal negotiations; counterparty sales performance |
| Credit reading | Match the cash-flow cliff against debt maturities and covenants |
| Equity reading | Risk-adjust forecasts by renewal probability; test the turnaround thesis against precedent (p.50) |
| Source | [Book] pp.49–50 |

## RQ09 — Key-person dependency

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | Revenue concentrated in a few doctors, faculty, founders or star employees; promoter-professionals; departures in the news |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Institutional brand strong enough to retain patients or clients. (b) Revenue follows the individual (book: three doctors, \~90% of IVF revenue, p.5). (c) Succession planned and contracted |
| Financial effects | Revenue at risk on exit; compensation renegotiation raises doctor or faculty cost, compressing sector gross margin (E2); transaction-triggered retention payments are debt-like (V3) |
| Possible outcomes | Revenue loss after a deal or departure; margin compression |
| Evidence — public | Key managerial personnel and related-party notes; revenue by specialty; departure news |
| Evidence — diligence | Agreements: lock-in, exclusivity, change-of-control, renewal (pp.6, 44–45); revenue by individual |
| Credit reading | Require lock-ins and key-person insurance; treat departure as an event of stress |
| Equity reading | Structure retention and earn-outs; discount for concentration |
| Source | [Book] pp.5–6, 44–45, 52 |

## RQ10 — Customer, payor or geography concentration

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | A top customer, country or distributor with a large revenue share; insurers, TPAs or government schemes as the dominant payor |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Strategic anchor relationships with low switching risk. (b) Dependency that hands pricing power to the counterparty (book: one country, 30% of exports through one distributor, p.57). (c) Payor-specific collection risk (book: \~60% TPA mix with disallowances, p.53) |
| Financial effects | Pricing pressure; gross-to-net leakage through disallowances (RQ04); receivables concentrated in one name; DSO stretch |
| Possible outcomes | Loss or renegotiation of a key account causing a revenue cliff; collection delays |
| Evidence — public | Customers above 10% of revenue (Ind AS 108); geographic segments; receivable ageing where disclosed |
| Evidence — diligence | Customer-wise revenue and receivables; contract tenor; payor-wise disallowance and collection history |
| Credit reading | Set concentration limits on receivable collateral; stress the loss of the top account |
| Equity reading | Discount for concentration; check that payor mix trends are sustainable |
| Source | [Book] pp.53, 57, 65 |

## RQ11 — Stable revenue with high churn

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | Flat recurring revenue alongside rising marketing spend or customer acquisition cost; customer counts with high gross losses |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Normal churn for the segment, matched by efficient acquisition. (b) A "leaky bucket": stable revenue only because acquisition replaces departures (book: 50% of a cohort gone by month 6, p.58). (c) Mix shift to lower-value customers |
| Financial effects | Marketing becomes a non-discretionary cost; revenue quality and durability fall; EBITDA depends on continued spend |
| Possible outcomes | Margin squeeze when acquisition cost rises; growth stalls |
| Evidence — public | Customer or subscriber counts and net additions; churn KPIs; marketing as % of revenue; acquisition cost trends (book method, p.43) |
| Evidence — diligence | Cohort revenue retention; acquisition cost by channel; lifetime value |
| Credit reading | Treat marketing as committed cost in DSCR; it cannot be switched off to service debt |
| Equity reading | Lower multiple for high churn even when revenue looks stable (p.58) |
| Source | [Book] pp.43, 58 |

## RQ12 — Volume growth from lower-quality revenue

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | Volume up sharply while average realisation falls (per student, per bed, per unit); growth from subsidised, quota or government-scheme customers; receivables rising faster than revenue |
| Competing hypotheses | (a) Deliberate expansion at a lower price point with sound unit economics. (b) Subsidised or quota-driven growth (book: enrolment +42% while revenue per student fell from 104k to 86k, p.51). (c) Discounting or scholarships to fill capacity. (d) Mix shift to cheaper courses or products |
| Financial effects | Revenue grows while cash lags (government receivables); margin dilution; working-capital strain |
| Possible outcomes | Collection delays; write-offs; cash conversion (R2) falls |
| Evidence — public | Realisation trend; payor or scheme mix; receivable ageing; revenue-to-cash gap (book: revenue 669 vs bank receipts 286, p.52) |
| Evidence — diligence | Course- or product-level unit economics; subsidy terms and collection history |
| Credit reading | Test cash conversion before giving credit for volume growth |
| Equity reading | Value on per-unit economics, not volume |
| Source | [Book] pp.51–52 |
