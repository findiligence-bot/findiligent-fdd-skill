# FinDiligent Sector Guide — Hotels

Version 1.0 · 21 Nov 2026 · Author: CA Ayush Bansal

The first sector KPI guide (D35). It has three parts:
- the operating KPIs a hotel company reports, and how to compute them
- how to read a move in those KPIs
- dated industry context notes, which a consuming project must verify against the company's own filings before relying on them (D26, D46)

Everything is indicative, not foolproof (D36). For sector gross margin, use the services row of E2 unless the company's direct-cost disclosure supports a different split.

## H1 — KPI definitions

| KPI | Formula | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Available room-nights | Rooms in inventory × days in period | Use operational rooms only; rooms under renovation are excluded where the company discloses them |
| Occupancy (%) | Room-nights sold ÷ available room-nights | |
| ARR (average room rate) | Room revenue ÷ room-nights sold | Called ADR in some disclosures |
| RevPAR (revenue per available room) | Room revenue ÷ available room-nights = Occupancy × ARR | The headline hotel KPI |
| Non-room revenue share | (F&B + banquets + other operating revenue) ÷ total revenue | Banquets and events (MICE) sit here |
| Managed vs owned mix | Fee income from managed or franchised hotels ÷ total revenue | Fee income carries little cost; owned hotels carry asset and lease costs |

Origin: [Claude, draft] (standard industry definitions; company definitions vary, so use the company's own where disclosed and say which).

## H2 — Reading a RevPAR move

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Decompose | Split RevPAR growth into occupancy and ARR. Growth from both is broader and more durable than growth from rate alone. Rate-led growth at flat or falling occupancy is a price-led signal (RQ02) |
| 2. Like-for-like | Separate same-hotel RevPAR from growth due to new rooms. New hotels ramp up from low occupancy, and acquisitions change the mix (RQ03) |
| 3. Demand mix | Split demand into domestic leisure, corporate travel, MICE, weddings and banquets, and foreign tourists. Each reacts to different drivers: airline capacity and airfares, geopolitics and visa policy, corporate budgets, the wedding calendar |
| 4. Non-room revenue | Check F&B and banquet revenue alongside rooms. Banquet demand can hold up a weak rooms quarter, and the reverse |
| 5. Seasonality | Compare quarters year on year, not sequentially. Q2 (Jul–Sep) is usually the seasonally weak quarter for Indian hotels, so a strong Q2 is notable. Read the September balance sheet with the W3 normal-level rule |
| 6. Cash and leases | Hotels are capital- and often lease-heavy. Apply the lease pairing rule (V4) and the capex split (R3); renovation capex deferred to protect cash is CC07 |

Origin: [Claude, draft].

## H3 — Industry context notes (dated, unverified)

Each note is a paraphrase of an industry reading the author shared. It is tagged unverified until checked against the companies' own Q2 filings and presentations. Archive a note after two quarters, as for hypotheses (D30).

### Q2 FY27 (Jul–Sep 2026)

- **Claim (unverified):** the best industry quarter for RevPAR in the last ten quarters. The growth came roughly equally from higher occupancy and higher room rates.
- **Drivers claimed:** domestic leisure travel, corporate demand, and events and banquets kept demand for rooms up. Fewer foreign tourists, because of higher airfares and geopolitics cutting airline capacity on some international routes, partly offset this.
- **How to use it:** treat it as a benchmark to compare a hotel company's Q2 FY27 KPIs against, not as evidence about that company. Check that company's occupancy and ARR split (H2 step 1). Check its exposure to foreign tourists, such as gateway-city or heritage-leisure portfolios. Check its banquet share (H2 step 4). A company lagging an industry-best quarter is itself a finding.
- **Source:** author note, 19 Nov 2026 (original source to be confirmed). [Author]
